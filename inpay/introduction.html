How I see my first mission at Inpay

What stands out to me about this mission?

Two hard problems at once — that's what makes it interesting.

Most companies either run a technical transformation or a culture change. Inpay is attempting both simultaneously: rewriting how software gets built while upskilling the people who build it. That's genuinely rare and genuinely hard.

What stands out most is the framing around upskilling. It signals that leadership understands this isn't a tooling problem — it's a thinking problem. Giving developers better hammers doesn't work if they're still solving everything like a nail. The mission is to change the mental model, not just the stack.

And the biggest lesson that aligns perfectly here: AI doesn't reduce work, it intensifies it. Poor context management leads to slower responses, degraded reasoning, and inconsistent results. The core challenge isn't "how do we use AI more" but "how do we use it well."

The goal isn't adoption metrics. It's durable capability that lives in the team long after the transformation ends.

What are the foreseeable risks?

Four risks worth watching:

Two-speed teams. Developers upskill at very different rates. Without deliberate design, you end up with a small group of AI-fluent developers pulling ahead while others feel left behind — or worse, feel surveilled. That splits team cohesion.

Context debt. Agentic systems live and die on context quality. If context hygiene isn't established early, the foundation will be shaky. The cost of fixing context management later is very high.

Wrong success metric. Lines of code shipped is not value delivered. If the wrong thing gets measured, teams will optimize for the wrong thing.

Demo-to-prod gap. Agents that impress in demos sometimes fail in production because real codebases have entropy. Watch for early wins that don't survive contact with actual squad workflows.

The biggest risk is invisible: organizational antibodies. Middle layers of management or senior developers who feel threatened will slow the transformation through inaction, not opposition.

What information is needed before starting?

Understand the system before trying to change it.

  • SDLC current state
  • Developer AI baseline
  • Definition of success
  • Biggest friction today
  • Early adopters map
  • Constraints that can't be touched

Before writing a single line of strategy, the current SDLC maturity needs to be understood — not what it looks like on paper, but how it actually works. Where do things slow down? Where do things get thrown over the wall?

Also essential: an honest baseline of developer AI literacy. Watching someone try to prompt an agent reveals more than any questionnaire.

Most critically: what does success look like in 90 days? And in 12 months? Alignment on that target matters, because every decision will be navigated toward it.

It also helps to know what's not allowed to be touched. Knowing constraints early saves expensive collisions later.

Breaking problems apart

Context boundaries first, then everything else.

Before decomposing any process, map the information flow. Who needs to know what, when, and in what form? Most problems that look like process problems are actually information problems in disguise.

Once the information map exists, three questions apply to every component:

  • What can be automated?
  • What must stay human?
  • What's the handoff protocol?

In agentic systems specifically, the focus is on where context gets lossy. That's where errors compound. A well-decomposed agentic workflow has explicit, minimal context passed between agents — not a giant shared state that every agent reads and writes.

It also helps to distinguish between essential complexity (the problem is genuinely hard) and accidental complexity (it was made harder than it needed to be). Attacking accidental complexity first almost always reveals a simpler essential problem underneath.

Decompose into units that can fail independently. If everything fails together, debugging becomes archaeology.

Choosing trade-offs

Reversibility beats speed, almost every time.

Better to ship something that can be undone than to lock in a bad pattern at scale. In a transformation context especially, the cost of a wrong architectural decision compounds — every developer who adopts the wrong pattern becomes technical debt to pay down later.

The trade-off for this kind of work:

  • Reversibility
  • Observability
  • Team adoptability
  • Speed
  • Feature completeness

Observability is especially important in agentic systems. If no one can see what an agent is doing, no one can debug it or trust it. Always trade some speed for a system that shows its work.

Team adoptability matters more than technical elegance. A slightly imperfect pattern that the whole team can use beats a perfect pattern that only one person can maintain.

When genuinely stuck on a trade-off, ask: "Which choice gives us more options later?" The answer usually breaks the tie.

Simplifying complexity

Find the constraint. Everything else is noise until that's fixed.

Most complex systems have one true bottleneck. Simplifying everything else before addressing the constraint is waste — local improvements with no system-wide impact. Constraint analysis comes before any redesign.

The second tool is language precision. Complexity often lives in ambiguous terms that mean different things to different people. "Agentic" means five different things to five developers. Establishing shared vocabulary early matters — not out of love for documentation, but because unclear terms create unclear systems.

Third: remove before adding. Every time there's a temptation to add something to simplify complexity, ask whether something can be removed instead. The simplest system is the one with fewer moving parts, not a more elegant arrangement of many parts.

In agentic SDLC, the single biggest simplification is a clear definition of "done" per agent task. Without that, agents loop, retry, and hallucinate — not because they're broken, but because they don't know when to stop.

Orchestrating agents

Like distributed systems, not magic boxes.

The biggest mistake is treating agents as capable of arbitrary task-completion. They're not. They're context processors with a limited window, probabilistic outputs, and no memory between calls unless explicitly provided. Orchestration design has to account for all three.

Core principles:

  • Explicit contracts between agents
  • Minimal context per agent
  • Human checkpoints at state transitions

Context budget management is the most underrated skill. Every agent in the pipeline gets only the context it needs — nothing more. Context pollution is the leading cause of inconsistent agent behavior. When a context window fills with irrelevant history, reasoning quality degrades fast.

Human checkpoints belong at state transitions — moments where the output of one agent becomes the input to the next. Not to slow things down, but because that's where error compounds if left unchecked.

Agents should fail loudly. A confident wrong answer is far more dangerous than an explicit "I don't know." Prompts and output schemas should surface uncertainty, not suppress it.

Working style

Asynchronously deep, synchronously aligned.

The best thinking happens in long uninterrupted blocks. Writing, prototyping, reading. Those blocks need protection — reserved for problems that actually need focused attention, not problems that just feel urgent.

Synchronous time — meetings, syncs — serves one purpose: course correction. Not detailed status updates, not ideation. Those work well async. Meetings should answer: "Are we still going in the right direction?"

Proactive communication matters. When something is blocked, stuck, or has changed, surface it — not to ask permission, but because the people around deserve the current picture, not a stale one.

In a transformation role, the job is to become unnecessary as fast as possible. Transfer capability into the team, not become the person everything flows through.

Handling ambiguity

Run small experiments to reduce it — not large deliberations.

Ambiguity is expensive when you try to resolve it in meetings. Time gets spent circling the same uncertainty without new information. It's almost always cheaper to run a small spike — a prototype, a pilot, a one-week test — and let reality give you data.

Two types of ambiguity matter here. The first is ambiguity about what to do — that requires alignment conversations with stakeholders. The second is ambiguity about how to do it — that requires experimentation, not more meetings.

In the context of this role specifically, expect ambiguity about what "agentic SDLC" means for Inpay specifically, what "upskilling" looks like in practice, how success gets measured. Move fast to reduce that ambiguity through direct conversations and small pilots, not long strategy documents.

Ambiguity is not the enemy. Moving forward while pretending ambiguity doesn't exist is the enemy. Name it, timebox it, and act within it.
